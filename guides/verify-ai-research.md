# How to verify an AI-generated answer before using it as evidence

**A practical research guide | Falah Mousa | 8 October 2026**

An AI answer can be useful for finding questions, identifying sources and organising information. It is not, by itself, proof that a statement is correct. A convincing explanation can still rely on an outdated report, a misleading statistic, a misquoted document or an invented reference.

This guide describes a repeatable review process. It applies to material about AI, technology, culture, society, economics and international affairs.

## 1. Write down the exact claim

Remove general language and identify what can actually be checked. A sentence such as “AI is transforming cultural work” is too broad to verify as a single claim.

A more useful question is: **Did a named institution introduce a particular AI system, for a defined purpose, during a documented period?** That creates a claim with a source, date and action to examine.

If an answer contains five factual claims, assess them separately. Do not mark the whole answer as reliable just because one citation is correct.

## 2. Open the original source

Follow every important reference. Check that the page or document exists and that its author, title and date match the reference. An AI answer may accurately name a genuine report but claim that the report says something it does not say.

Distinguish an original study, legal text, institutional decision or dataset from a press release or commentary about that source. If only a secondary account is available, attribute the finding to that account and explain the limit.

## 3. Confirm dates, definitions and denominators

Ask what period the claim covers, where the figures come from and how the underlying terms are defined.

For example, suppose an AI answer says **“80% of readers now prefer digital books.”** Before repeating it, identify the survey population, country, sample size, fieldwork date, the definition of *prefer*, and whether the question was about preference, recent use or purchasing. These measures are not interchangeable.

Never turn a forecast into a recorded result or a proposal into a decision already implemented.

## 4. Look for conflicting evidence

Search for another credible source with a different method or relevant perspective. Two websites repeating the same announcement do not necessarily provide two independent confirmations.

Record conflicts explicitly. If two surveys disagree, examine how each selected participants and phrased its questions. Do not average incompatible results simply to produce one confident number.

## 5. Check quotes, translations and context

When material crosses languages, retain the original wording beside the translation where permissions allow. Pay attention to words such as *may*, *must*, *planned*, *adopted*, and *implemented*.

For Arabic-to-English source reviews, institutional terms and the difference between an allegation and an official finding can materially change the meaning. Note where an interpretation remains uncertain.

## 6. Separate findings from interpretation

Use distinct labels:

- **Established:** The claim is supported by appropriate dated evidence.
- **Attribution required:** A source or author makes the claim, but it is not independently demonstrated.
- **Conflicting evidence:** Credible sources disagree or measure different things.
- **Not established:** The information available is insufficient.

Then write your interpretation separately. “The ministry announced a pilot on 5 May” is a factual statement to verify; “the pilot will improve public services” is a prediction requiring different evidence.

## 7. State the limits and the cutoff date

Finish with a brief account of what you could not confirm, which documents you could not access, and the date through which you checked developments. Readers need to know whether your findings describe an earlier period or the current position.

Do not upload confidential client documents, unreleased manuscripts or personal records to a public repository or an unapproved external AI service.

## An example of a compact evidence record

| Field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Research question | Did Institution A launch an AI translation service in 2026? |
| AI-generated claim | “The service was launched nationwide in April 2026.” |
| Original source | Official announcement and technical documentation, if available |
| What to check | Announcement date; whether this is a pilot or a full launch; locations covered |
| Status | **Not established** until the original documents are examined |
| Limit | No conclusion about service quality without performance evidence |

The example above is **hypothetical**, not a claim about a real institution.
