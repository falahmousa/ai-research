# Reading, evidence and source verification

This method is for researchers who use nonfiction books to develop questions that must then be checked against reliable evidence.

## Keep five categories separate

1. **Bibliographic facts.** Who wrote the book, who published it, which edition, and when? Verify against the publisher or library record.
2. **The author's argument.** State the claim as the author's interpretation, not as an independently established fact.
3. **The book's factual examples.** Locate the underlying primary document, official data, court ruling or scholarly study.
4. **Your analysis.** Explain what follows from the evidence, which interpretation is plausible, and which alternatives remain.
5. **Limits and updates.** Record the date of the evidence. An earlier election assessment cannot automatically establish a later election's outcome.

## A six-step workflow

1. Formulate one research question before collecting titles.
2. Search publisher catalogues, library records and relevant books; note edition and year.
3. Record the most relevant proposition in your own words, with an exact page reference **only when you read the original edition**. Otherwise, label a summary as a secondary account.
4. Locate corroborating and competing evidence independent of the book.
5. Classify each claim: supported, attributed to an author, contested or not established.
6. Write a dated brief that separates findings from analysis and includes a concise limitations section.

## Copyright and responsible reading

Book descriptions, catalogue records and short bibliographic facts can help discover reading material. Access to a commercial reading service does not grant permission to publish its full summaries, audio or proprietary text. Do not scrape Blinkist or other subscription services. Do not describe a book as completely read when only a condensed summary was examined. Use brief quotations only when justified and legally permissible; this repository reproduces none.
