# Can an elected government weaken democracy?

**Books & Evidence | Falah Mousa | 8 October 2026**

An election gives a government authority to govern for a limited period. It does not give that government authority to make future elections unfair or to prevent courts, journalists and legislatures from examining its actions. The relevant distinction is between exercising an electoral mandate and changing the conditions under which political opponents can compete.

This brief is an original comparison of published arguments and publicly available evidence. It is **not a quotation or reproduction of a Blinkist summary**, and it does not treat a book's interpretation as proof of a factual claim.

## 1. What the books help us ask

**Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt, _How Democracies Die_ (2018).** Their comparative framework focuses on the importance of political parties and informal rules that limit the use of formally legal powers. A useful question for any reform is whether it strengthens public accountability or makes it easier for the governing party to escape scrutiny. This is an analytical framework rather than evidence that a particular reform had a particular effect.

**Anne Applebaum, _Twilight of Democracy_ (2020).** Applebaum examines why some educated political and cultural elites become willing to support authoritarian arguments. This expands the inquiry beyond individual leaders and voters to the people who defend official decisions, shape public information and approve changes to institutions. Her explanations remain the author's interpretation, and should be tested against specific cases.

**Cas Mudde and Cristóbal Rovira Kaltwasser, _Populism: A Very Short Introduction_ (2017).** Their definition treats populism as a *thin-centred ideology* organised around the claim that politics should express the will of a supposedly unified people against a supposedly corrupt elite. Populist arguments can appear on the political left or right. A claim to speak for the people does not make opponents or minorities less entitled to participate in public life. [Publisher and chapter information](https://academic.oup.com/book/866).

**Benjamin Carter Hett, _The Death of Democracy_ (2018).** Hett's historical account of the Weimar Republic directs attention to economic crises, political violence, decisions by conservative elites and the use of emergency powers. The collapse of Weimar democracy is a particular historical case, not a ready-made equivalence with contemporary states.

These four books approach related questions from different perspectives. None, on its own, can establish the current condition of a country's elections or courts.

## 2. Hungary: the difference between voting and fair competition

The OSCE Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights assessed Hungary's parliamentary election and referendum of **3 April 2022**. Its final report found that voting was well run, but that the campaign lacked a level playing field. It identified an overlap between government and ruling-coalition messaging and said biased news coverage limited voters' ability to make an informed choice. Opposition parties could largely campaign, but the assessment recorded important inequalities in the campaign environment.

**Evidence:** [ODIHR final report, 29 July 2022](https://odihr.osce.org/odihr/elections/523568) and [ODIHR summary of findings](https://odihr.osce.org/odihr/elections/523565).

**What this establishes:** international observers documented problems with the fairness of campaign conditions in a specific election. **What it does not establish by itself:** the exact legal or institutional condition of every Hungarian election before or after 2022. Later elections require their own dated evidence.

## 3. Why party loyalty matters

Matthew Graham and Milan Svolik tested how voters respond when candidates take positions that violate democratic principles. Their study used a candidate-choice experiment and evidence from a 2017 US congressional election. They found that many participants were willing to place party, ideology or policy preferences above democratic principles, particularly in polarised settings.

**Evidence:** [Graham and Svolik, _American Political Science Review_ (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055420000052).

This research concerns the United States. It should not be presented as a measured finding about every European electorate. Its broader research question, however, is relevant elsewhere: will a party's supporters oppose a decision that benefits their side but makes future political competition less fair?

## 4. Germany: an important historical distinction

In January 1933, Adolf Hitler became German chancellor through appointment, not because his party had won an outright electoral majority. The subsequent destruction of pluralist politics involved coercion, restrictions on opponents and the Enabling Act. Treating this simply as a case of voters directly electing a dictator would be historically misleading.

The episode is relevant to the decisions made by established political elites when they believe they can use an anti-democratic movement's popularity while retaining control. Comparisons with contemporary governments need careful attention to the very different historical circumstances.

**Further reading:** Hett, _The Death of Democracy_ (2018), and Levitsky and Ziblatt, _How Democracies Die_ (2018). A historical book is background reading here; a formal legal or documentary account should be consulted for detailed claims about the Enabling Act and the appointment process.

## 5. Practical questions for researchers

Before declaring that a government has changed democratic competition, examine:

1. **Authority:** Which law, decree, appointment or institutional rule changed, and when did it take effect?
2. **Oversight:** Can courts, parliament and independent agencies still review decisions and enforce remedies?
3. **Competition:** Can political opponents organise, campaign, receive coverage and contest results on fair terms?
4. **Evidence:** Is a claim supported by an official text, an independent observer, an academic study or just a political statement?
5. **Scope:** Is the conclusion limited to one election, one institution or a documented period?
6. **Counter-evidence:** What findings dispute or qualify the initial interpretation?

## 6. Conclusion

Elections are necessary to democracy, but they are not its only safeguard. The better test is whether citizens retain a genuine opportunity to challenge officials, organise opposition and replace the government under fair rules. A research assessment should establish which specific rules and practices have changed, distinguish evidence from interpretation, and state the limits of available information.
